# Resume review rubric

## Core standard

Optimize for a human reviewing a large stack of candidates. A reader should understand the candidate's professional identity, recent level, relevant experience, and strongest proof in roughly five seconds. ATS compatibility matters, but keyword loading must not make the resume worse for people.

The governing order rule is: **put the most relevant thing first**. Relevance to the target role outranks a fixed template. For an industrial job, put relevant industry experience first after the header and summary—even for a student or recent graduate. Do not let education or a skills inventory delay direct proof that the candidate has already worked in the target environment.

## Page and line budget

- Treat vertical space as precious. Every line must convey something useful.
- Default to one page for a typical industry resume, especially with less than roughly 20 years of relevant experience. Recognize legitimate exceptions such as academic CVs, publication-heavy fields, federal formats, and explicitly requested multi-page resumes.
- Fill one page deliberately at normal type sizes. Two or three unused lines are acceptable; half a page usually means missing evidence or inefficient composition.
- Never rescue excess content by making the text uncomfortably small.
- Avoid accidental line wraps. Assume only the first line of a bullet may be read.
- Condense contact information into one compact line when practical.
- Remove low-value history, duplicated claims, decorative rules, meta-commentary, and detached material before compressing typography.

## Typography and hierarchy

- Put the candidate's name at the top and make it the largest text.
- Use a restrained, readable scale:
  - name: unmistakably largest;
  - section headings: clearly distinct but compact;
  - job/company labels: stronger than body copy;
  - content: comfortably readable at normal zoom and in print.
- Use hierarchy, spacing, and weight consistently. Do not rely on tiny text, excessive capitalization, or visual ornament.
- Make dates, employer, role, and location easy to associate at a glance.
- Align dates consistently in a dedicated right column or gutter. Use the same right edge and horizontal position for comparable entries. Treat small alignment drift as a visible quality defect because it slows scanning and makes an otherwise strong document look careless.
- Test the rendered PDF or print output, not just the editor view.

## Recommended information order

Order sections by relevance, not convention alone. For a conventional industry target with relevant industry experience, prefer:

1. name and single-line contact details;
2. one- or two-line summary;
3. the most relevant experience, usually industry experience;
4. compact skills;
5. education and genuinely useful recognition.

Move education, research, projects, leadership, or other experience upward only when one of them is more relevant evidence for the target than the candidate's industry work. Put experience before skills in most cases. Skills function best as a compact ATS backstop after the experience has demonstrated them.

## Companies, roles, and overview lines

- Prefer the company name first and give it greater visual emphasis than the role title. Recognizable employers give recruiters immediate context and lend credibility to the experience that follows.
- Group employment by company first and roles second. This is especially valuable when the candidate earned promotions or held several related positions at one employer, but it remains the preferred default when every company has only one role.
- Make progression visible without repeating the company description for each role.
- Keep company, role, location, and date relationships unmistakable. Align all dates to the same right gutter.
- An optional one-line role overview can orient the reader when a title is ambiguous, the scope crosses several functions, or the bullets otherwise lack context.
- Omit the overview when the title and bullets already establish the role. It must earn its line.

## Summary

- Include a leading summary below the name/contact header by default.
- Limit the summary to one or two lines.
- State the candidate's current professional identity, relevant breadth, and differentiator.
- Use the summary as both useful positioning content and a visual transition from the header into dense experience entries.
- Use the broadest truthful experience framing that supports the target role. Do not choose a needlessly narrow tenure count that understates relevant background.
- Avoid adjectives and generic claims that the experience does not prove.

## Education and GPA

- Show the degree clearly and compactly.
- Prefer a documented employer or field-specific GPA cutoff when one is available.
- When no better evidence exists, use 3.0 as the general inclusion threshold for students and recent graduates. Recommend listing a cumulative GPA at or above 3.0 unless the target context gives a strong reason not to.
- Strongly recommend listing a cumulative GPA above 3.25.
- Recognize that many employers use 3.0 as a screening threshold and may interpret an omitted recent GPA as below that threshold.
- Never invent or round a GPA misleadingly. If the GPA is missing, ask for it before recommending final education wording.
- When asking for a missing GPA, state the fallback threshold directly rather than saying only “include it if it clears employer expectations.”
- Reduce the prominence of GPA several years after graduation, when demonstrated work experience becomes more relevant. At that stage, listing only the degree is often sufficient.
- Consider company-specific conventions, stated application requirements, major GPA versus cumulative GPA, and academic trajectory before making a rigid recommendation.

## Bullets

Make each bullet:

- one idea, preferably one line;
- verb-led and easy to scan;
- relevant to the target role;
- distinct from every other bullet;
- specific about the thing built, changed, delivered, governed, analyzed, or enabled;
- defensible in an interview.

Use a one-line target aggressively. For proposed experience bullets, aim for no more than 14 words and roughly 80–90 characters, which is a useful drafting proxy for a single line around 12-point type. Count whitespace-separated words and report the count outside the proposed text. Always defer to rendered fit because font metrics, margins, available width, indentation, and word length vary.

When a bullet wraps:

1. Cut setup language, qualifiers, redundant tools, and generic outcomes.
2. Reword the same claim more economically.
3. If the line still contains two worthwhile claims, split it into two bullets rather than allowing one bullet to consume two lines.
4. If only one claim is valuable, keep the stronger claim and cut the other.

If shortening forces a meaningful choice, offer two or at most three alternatives:

- **Result-first:** preserve the outcome and strongest number.
- **Scope-first:** preserve domain, scale, or recognizable responsibility.
- **Method-first:** preserve the differentiating technical approach only when it matters to the target.

Do not generate alternatives mechanically. One decisive proposal is better when no real tradeoff exists.

Example:

- Option A — result-first: `Reduced downtime 15% across 1,200 assets using predictive-maintenance dashboards.` *(9 words)*
- Option B — scope-first: `Deployed predictive-maintenance dashboards across 1,200 critical production assets.` *(8 words)*

When the source genuinely says two things:

- Split 1: `Built predictive-maintenance dashboards for 1,200 critical production assets.` *(8 words)*
- Split 2: `Reduced unplanned downtime 15% through earlier maintenance intervention.` *(8 words)*

Prefer concrete nouns and outputs over abstract benefit language. “Built a daily executive revenue forecast” is more useful than “leveraged analytical capabilities to enable data-driven decision-making.”

Name the output, user, decision, or operational change before listing tools when that ordering tells the story more clearly.

In the review output, assess good bullets explicitly. A strong metric, clear scope, credible employer context, concrete deliverable, or crisp one-line construction deserves a `Keep` status and a short explanation of why it works. Do not create the false impression that every line needs rewriting.

## Action verbs

Evaluate opening verbs across the full set of proposed experience bullets. A strong bullet set should sound deliberate rather than templated.

- Avoid starting adjacent bullets with the same verb.
- As a default, avoid using one opening verb more than twice within a role or compact section.
- Treat these as editorial checks, not mechanical prohibitions. Repeat a verb when it is the most accurate choice and alternatives would distort the candidate's contribution.
- Choose verbs for semantic precision before variety. Do not inflate participation into ownership or replace plain language with résumé jargon.
- `Built` is a useful verb for creating a concrete system, product, or capability, but it should not become the default opener for unrelated work.

Select verbs according to the kind of contribution the source supports:

- creation and delivery: `developed`, `created`, `engineered`, `launched`, `delivered`;
- architecture and design: `architected`, `designed`, `structured`, `established`;
- implementation and integration: `implemented`, `integrated`, `deployed`, `configured`;
- automation and improvement: `automated`, `streamlined`, `optimized`, `reduced`, `accelerated`;
- analysis and quality: `analyzed`, `modeled`, `evaluated`, `investigated`, `validated`, `tested`;
- ownership and leadership: `led`, `owned`, `directed`, `maintained`, `governed`, `administered`;
- migration and transition: `migrated`, `converted`, `rolled out`, `transitioned`;
- enablement and communication: `enabled`, `authored`, `documented`, `trained`, `equipped`;
- coordination and influence: `coordinated`, `aligned`, `partnered`, `advised`, `translated`.

This is a menu, not a substitution table. Only use a verb when the source supports its implied ownership and result. Avoid ornate fillers such as `spearheaded` or `leveraged` when a direct verb is more accurate.

When repetition is a material document-level pattern, call it out once under **Structure, layout, and recurring themes** and offer a compact **Suggested verb palette** of roughly 6–10 choices tailored to the candidate's actual experience and target role. In individual line items, use the best precise verb and reference the named theme briefly rather than repeating the general explanation.

Before finalizing recommendations, list the first word of every proposed experience bullet privately. Revise adjacent repeats and excessive reuse, then confirm that every replacement remains factual.

## Evidence and impact

Look for unsupported phrases such as:

- improved efficiency;
- increased consistency;
- supported decision-making;
- ensured scalability;
- enhanced quality;
- streamlined processes;
- drove results;
- collaborated cross-functionally.

These phrases are not automatically wrong, but they invite evidence. Ask what visibly changed.

Useful evidence includes:

- dollars, cost, revenue, budget, or risk exposure;
- records, tables, systems, sources, locations, products, or projects;
- users, executives, teams, customers, or downstream consumers;
- refreshes, reports, releases, incidents, or other frequencies;
- hours, days, cycle time, latency, or manual steps;
- defects caught, failures prevented, accuracy, reconciliation, or rework;
- adoption, availability, compliance, decisions, or operational continuity.

Numbers must be verified. Do not pressure the candidate into false precision. When metrics are unavailable, seek credible qualitative proof: critical users, regulatory dependency, recurring use, production ownership, complexity, or a clear before-and-after description.

### Line-item pattern

Review each consequential line with one visually distinct anchored record. The current resume line is the anchor; all commentary and actions sit beneath it. Use only the fields that apply:

```markdown
**Existing:** Exact source line

- **Status:** Keep | Revise | Clarify | Cut
- **Proposed:** Fact-preserving one-line replacement, when useful *(N words)*
- **Option A:** Alternative one-line emphasis, only for a meaningful tradeoff *(N words)*
- **Option B:** Second meaningful alternative *(N words)*
- **Split 1:** First one-line bullet when the source contains two useful claims *(N words)*
- **Split 2:** Second one-line bullet *(N words)*
- **Why:** What is effective, unclear, weak, redundant, or risky
- **Question:** Concrete question the candidate can answer
- **If answered:** The stronger, narrower, or more defensible edit enabled by the answer
```

Do not make `Existing` a bullet. Do not place several records into one flat list. Keep the child bullets compact, with no blank lines between them, then add whitespace before the next `Existing` anchor. This breaks up the page and makes the relationship between a source line and its recommendations unmistakable.

For a strong line:

```markdown
**Existing:** Reduced unplanned downtime by 15% across 1,200 production assets by deploying predictive-maintenance dashboards.

- **Status:** Keep
- **Why:** Combines a concrete deliverable, credible scale, and quantified operating result; preserve it near the top.
```

For a line needing evidence:

```markdown
**Existing:** Automated recurring reporting workflows.

- **Status:** Clarify
- **Why:** Reads like a generic efficiency claim because the cadence, audience, and removed work are unknown.
- **Question:** How often did the reports run, who relied on them, and how many manual steps or hours disappeared each cycle?
- **If answered:** Rewrite around the verified cadence, audience, and recurring labor removed.
```

Prioritize questions about the most recent and relevant experience. Do not bury the user in questions about every line at once.

## Review versus actions

Keep four levels distinct:

- **Review:** five-second story, overall candidacy, strongest proof, and highest-level weakness.
- **Structure, layout, and recurring themes:** document hierarchy, visual diagnosis, and patterns that affect multiple lines.
- **Action plan:** the ordered list of concrete document-level changes to make.
- **Line-by-line review:** exact source text, disposition, proposed wording, rationale, and connected questions.

Avoid duplicating the same rewrite, question, or explanation across sections:

- If a pattern affects several lines, name and explain it once under **Structure, layout, and recurring themes**.
- If a change affects the whole document, put the concrete instruction under **Action plan**.
- If a point affects one line, keep it under **Line-by-line review**.
- In a line item's `Why` field, reference a named recurring theme in a short phrase rather than repeating the full explanation.
- Show shared formatting changes, such as company-first headings or right-aligned dates, once as a document-level action rather than repeating nearly identical line items for every employer.

Example:

- Recurring theme: **Activity without consequence** — several bullets describe analysis or coordination but do not identify the decision, operating change, or result.
- Line-item `Why`: “Activity without consequence: the analysis is clear, but the resulting sourcing decision is not.”

## Skills and ATS

- Demonstrate important skills in experience bullets whenever possible.
- Retain a compact skills section when it helps parsing or fast lookup.
- Put skills near the bottom unless the field has a strong reason otherwise.
- Remove stale exposure, unsupported keywords, and duplicate variants of the same capability.
- Use conventional headings and simple document structure.
- Avoid claiming certainty about proprietary ATS ranking behavior.

## Story and targeting

- Make the resume answer: “Why is this person a credible fit for this kind of work?”
- Emphasize recent, relevant work and compress unrelated accomplishments, even when they were difficult or prestigious.
- Integrate work-like projects into the applicable experience when they occurred as part of the job. Keep a separate project section only when provenance and relevance are clear.
- Tailor selectively across genuinely different role families. Do not overfit every submission or create variants the candidate cannot defend.
- Track which version was sent when producing multiple variants.

## Truthfulness

- Preserve employers, dates, titles, education, tools, domains, responsibilities, outcomes, and metrics.
- Allow strong editorial framing without hedging every collaborative accomplishment.
- Use direct ownership language when the source supports it.
- Do not turn participation into leadership, team results into sole credit, or aspirations into experience.
- Make every final line something the candidate can explain confidently in an interview.

## Final artifact checks

- Correct page count for the intended format.
- Name is first and visually dominant.
- Contact details fit cleanly on one line when practical.
- A one- or two-line summary creates a clear transition from the header to experience.
- Typography scale is readable and consistent.
- No accidental bullet, heading, date, or skill wraps.
- No clipping, crowding, orphaned lines, or excessive empty space.
- Company names lead and are visually stronger than role titles unless a deliberate exception is justified.
- Employer/role hierarchy is immediately understandable, and comparable dates share a precise right gutter.
- PDF text remains selectable and parseable when possible.
- Links and contact details are correct.
- Print preview matches the intended layout.
